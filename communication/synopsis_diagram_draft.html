The operational side of the bridge

The structural side. We start with type-theoretica foundationsb — a way of doing mathematics in which mathematical objects are defined by the relationships between them, rather than by what they are made of. When we look at how such a foundation reasons internallyX — what its semanticsc is — the structures we find behave like field theoriesd: local data on a space, with rules for how the local pieces fit together globally.

The operational side. We also study field-theoretica dynamicsb — local-to-global processes evolving over a space, with their own rules of change. When such a process stabilizes — when its long-time behaviour settles — what it leaves behindc is itself a type theoryd: an internalX language of mathematical objects and the maps between them.

The structural side of the bridgeModal type theory as a system of perspectives

Modal type theory adds modalities — operators that filter, reflect, or restate parts of a structure — to a type-theoretic foundation. The result is a foundation in which different perspectives on the same structure can be made formally precise.

Inspiration

Mid-twentieth-century mathematics underwent a silent revolution, led in different registers by William Lawvere (1937–2023) and Alexander Grothendieck (1928–2014). Mathematics moved from defining objects by their internal construction — “what is this set?” — to defining them by their relationships — “how does this object map to others?” — and from doing mathematics in one fixed logic to recognising that each context — each topos — carries its own internal logic. The shift was from substance to relation, and from one logic to many. Lawvere's particular contribution was cohesion: a context-internal distinction between continuous variation and discrete points — the basis of the project's cohesive ∞-topos framework.

Philosophy made a structurally similar move in the same period, finding roots in a lineage traced by Gilles Deleuze: from Duns Scotus and Spinoza, through Leibniz and Nietzsche, to Peirce and Whitehead. This family replaced the search for a single absolute truth with the recognition that reality is irreducibly multi-perspectival, each perspective carrying valid inputs of its own.

Both movements — the mathematical and the philosophical — arrive at the same embrace of multiplicity. The link between them is structural, not historical: independent disciplines reaching the same perspective.

Hypothesis

The modal context must be complex enough to support reasoning across many levels of structure at once. Yuri Manin (1937–2023), a direct intellectual descendent of Grothendieck, gave a reading of complexity not as randomness, not as regularity, but as generative depth — the kind a setting has when it supports multi-level adjoint reasoning. The hypothesis on the structural side: modal cohesion meets this threshold, and so the foundation has enough depth for complexity to actually develop in it; shallower modal structures cannot host this kind of growth.

The operational side of the bridgeConcurrency as implementable protocol

Concurrency studies systems of agents acting in parallel and communicating with each other. Two ideas anchor the modern study of such systems: the actor model (which describes the agents) and session types (which describe the structured communication between them).

Inspiration

One of Grothendieck's gifts to mathematics was his radical generalization of the notion of a cover — a collection of local views that together describe a whole, like an atlas mapping a country in patches. Each patch makes sense on its own; the way patches overlap is part of the structure. In his memoirs, Récoltes et Semailles, Grothendieck meditates on how mathematical insight grows from local perspectives and the bridges between them; in Esquisse d'un Programme, he sketches the structural directions he believed could carry that growth further.

Operational layers

The protocol the project builds separates communication into two layers:

  • “How” layer. How participants communicate — who talks to whom, in what order, with what kind of messages (question, request…). The protocol of the conversation itself.
  • “What” layer. What they communicate about — typed content carrying meaning, structured so every receiver can decode it. The substance of the conversation.

The how-layer is what lets a community of arbitrary participants do something together without imposing a centralized authority on them. The what-layer is what allows their output to be meaningful, readable, reusable and tractable in a network of such protocols.

Hypothesis

The protocol must follow strict separation of concerns. The system's organisation is built as a cascade: each layer's resolution by modular separation opens the next problem; separation of concerns is the recursive move that produces the whole structure.

The cross-side bridgeHow the structural and operational sides constitute each other

Between modal type theory (the structural side) and concurrency (the operational side), there is a two-way bridge. The two sides are mutually constitutive: each makes the other possible.

Allow and Require

The bridge has two directions:

  • Allow: The modal context, by making multi-perspective reasoning formally precise and meaningful, allows concurrent agents to operate at a level of complexity which justifies the structural investments built into the system. Without this permission, the protocol would be better off collapsing to a hierarchy. In return, the concurrent operations are part of an evolution which culminated in this modal context for agents.
  • Require: The concurrent protocol, to separate its concerns cleanly, requires the modal context to host its operations. And without this requirement, the modal foundation would have no praxis — no community of agents actually doing the work.

Mutually constitutive

The structural and operational sides are parent and child to each other — in turn, depending on the direction. The structural side is parent to the operational side when the operational side's separations need a multi-perspective context to live in. The operational side is parent to the structural side when the structural side's modal structure needs an actual community of agents to mean anything in practice. Together they act on their shared context like an ecosystem: each one's activity becomes the other's environment, and the environment's state is what the next round acts in.

Iteration

Once this relation is established, it can be repeated. Each round of the bridge produces a new (context, protocol) pair, in which new structures become statable that were not statable before.

The two sides are not symmetric: the structural side is settled enough that its perspectives can be re-applied; the operational side is fluid enough that each application produces genuinely new structure. This asymmetry is what keeps the iteration alive.

The sequence of stages produced this way is the developing protocol the implementation targets.

What it outputs

The same algebraic structure that organises quantum field theories — the cobordism hypothesis — organises the project's session-typed protocols. The framework's output is a kind of field theory: a way of assigning local structure to local pieces of a space, with global coherence emerging from how the pieces fit together. This is why the top sentence of the synopsis holds: type-theoretic foundations, when their internal semantics is read off carefully, produce field-theoretic structure. And it is why the bottom sentence holds: field-theoretic dynamics, when stabilised, produce an internal type theory. The two halves of the project meet at this point.
