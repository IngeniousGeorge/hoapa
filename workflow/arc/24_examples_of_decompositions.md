# Examples of decompositions

## Summary of conversations with Claude and Codex (Codex Astra)

The strongest result of the two conversations is this: a separation of concerns becomes productive through the relations it makes possible, and those relations can themselves become objects of further work. Computation, neural AI, and nervous systems show different ways this happens. They support Hoapa’s investigation of how an achievement becomes a condition for subsequent achievement. They also make the dedication thesis more demanding: we must examine what the interactions between substrate and representation accomplish, rather than require them to remain physically separate.

1. What survives of the main argument

The distinction between synthetic and analytic remains useful, provided we identify what is being decomposed and at which level. In Hoapa’s reading of Lautman, the synthetic moment supplies a common plan through which different contributions become expressible; the analytic moment articulates the particular achievement into its own constituents. These are ways of organizing understanding, not permanent classifications of hardware, software, neurons, or people.

The examples show why a strict physical division cannot carry the whole argument. Learning changes the machinery through which later learning happens. Compiling a program turns a description into something executable. A mathematical construction can become the setting of another construction. The separation remains intelligible while its implementation changes.

Dedication therefore concerns the purpose of the relation: its transformations should serve the production, application, and continued possibility of representations and algorithms. Maintenance becomes an explicit condition of this ambition. The biological examples establish that such interdependence exists; they do not establish that every biological mechanism serves it perfectly, or prove Hoapa’s normative requirement.

2. How to use the three examples

They work best as comparisons along several dimensions, rather than a progression from primitive computers to advanced brains.

| Example | Common framework | Particular organization developed within it | What makes it useful to the argument |
|---|---|---|---|
| Explicitly programmed computation | An execution model and its interfaces | Programs, data structures, procedures | The division of responsibilities is deliberately exposed and comparatively easy to inspect. |
| Trained neural AI | A designed architecture, numerical operations, and training procedure | Learned parameters and representations | An operational organization can be acquired through work and then support further work. |
| Nervous systems within organisms | Developing physiological organization and signalling mechanisms | Learned skills, patterns of activity, and changing connections | Learning, activity, and maintenance interact within a living organization. |

These columns give selected descriptions, not exhaustive decompositions.

Ordinary computation is the clearest engineered example, because interfaces specify which physical differences may be ignored for a computation. Its cleanliness is an accomplishment of engineering. It does not require identical energy costs for every bit, or hardware untouched by execution. Computers also already have elaborate recursions: compilers, interpreters, operating systems, and programs treated as data.

Neural AI adds learned organization within that engineered setting. In the familiar training/inference arrangement, training produces parameters that later activity uses. Other arrangements permit continued adaptation, so frozen weights should not become part of the definition of AI.

The nervous system is a particularly rich example of coupling. Its support comes from the organism and its environment; the brain does not maintain itself independently. Calling it “the richest example for this discussion” is defensible. Calling it the endpoint of a hierarchy of advancement would add a claim the argument does not need.

3. What the neuron discussion actually established

Neurons offer a useful analogy for participation through shared interfaces and for distributed representation. They do not supply a biological instance of equal standing. Cells differ substantially, particular populations can be crucial to particular memories, and selection affects the substrate itself. For example, experiments have shown selective recruitment of neurons into a memory trace through differences in CREB activity. Han and colleagues, 2007

Nevertheless, reinforcement need not represent a second activity competing with representational work. Changing connections can be how the system acquires its operative organization. This is the useful distinction behind “dopamine teaches rather than pays,” although that slogan cannot summarize dopamine’s many functions.

The correction is that wiring changes through more than learning: development and homeostatic regulation also matter. Experimental work on synaptic scaling shows how activity regulates connection strengths to stabilize neuronal activity. Consequently, “a brain whose wiring changes only through what it learns” would be an indefensible replacement for the original neuron parenthesis. Turrigiano and colleagues, 1998

The sentence “as neurons are dedicated to thought” should therefore disappear. The brain earns its place through a more interesting observation: activities that produce knowledge can also reorganize the conditions of further cognition.

4. What “the analytic aspires to become synthetic” gains from these examples

The strongest formulation is:

> What one episode develops as a particular achievement can become part of the common framework through which later work proceeds.

A compiler is a constructed program that enables further programs. A trained model is an acquired organization that enables subsequent inference. An acquired skill changes what later tasks make available to its practitioner. Mathematical definitions and constructions can become the language in which new problems are posed.

This is a change of role relative to subsequent activity. The achievement remains something produced by earlier work while also functioning as a condition for later work. Nothing requires neurons to begin as a knowledge graph, or abstract knowledge to turn literally into new biological material.

Consciousness can remain an open question beyond these examples. Learning, acquired skills, and reusable computational organization already establish the phenomenon we need to investigate.

We should also distinguish three things: repeated use of an existing framework, modification of its procedures, and renewal of what the framework makes expressible. Feedback or autoregression establishes the first kind of continuation readily; it does not by itself establish the third.

5. The precise contribution of Miguel Moreno’s work

Moreno and his coauthors provide a mathematical bridge between neural computation, logic, and distributed computation. Their current result characterizes a specified class of floating-point encoder–decoder transformers without positional encodings through a temporal logic and distributed automata. This supports examining a neural architecture through explicit rules of interaction and expressibility. Ahvonen, Heiman, Kuusisto, Moreno, and Selin

It does not explain how training acquires representations, prove that training selects a unique formula, or establish the autonomy of the resulting system. It characterizes a class of computational models under stated assumptions.

There is also a useful restraint in the paper: under its assumptions, autoregressive generation exhibits eventual periodicity and admits a finitary logical characterization. An output becoming another input need not create a new expressive world. Moreno’s work consequently supplies both a bridge and a control for Hoapa’s stronger interpretation.

6. How the movement connects to borrow–work–replay

The combined insight is more precise than “separation followed by mixture produces replay”:

> A reusable achievement needs enough independence from its origin to travel, and enough explicit connection to its conditions to work elsewhere.

Separation helps expose responsibilities and dependencies. Integration makes their cooperation possible. Concrete difficulties can demand bridges, shared structures, or new separations. Their success is measured by what subsequent work can actually do with the result.

A good borrow anticipates replay by keeping relevant dependencies visible. It does not anticipate the future result. In natural deduction, a derivation of B under an assumption A can yield A → B by discharging A; any other undischarged assumptions remain dependencies. Later application with an appropriate A is the replay. The form of the original borrowing makes this possible.

This gives the retrospective reading its force: we can recognize in a successful replay what the original borrowing needed to preserve. There is no retrocausality, and no guarantee of usefulness for every future purpose.

Efficiency alone does not entail BWR. A system can complete an immediate task by exhausting its resources or making its output dependent on its producer. Hoapa adds the requirement that achievements remain available for further work while sustaining its conditions. That requirement makes replay a design concern from the beginning.

7. What Simondon contributes

The two conversations identified several complementary connections.

- Concretization describes functions becoming mutually supporting within a technical object. A cooling fin can also reinforce an engine component. This helps explain how functional distinctions can remain intelligible while their implementation becomes integrated. Simondon’s “abstract” and “concrete” here concern technical organization; they cannot simply replace Hoapa’s abstract/concrete vocabulary. On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects, translated extract
- Transduction gives the closest account of an achievement becoming a condition for subsequent achievement: constituted structure guides further structuring. It also allows the process to establish new distinctions, rather than merely combine already finished parts. “The Position of the Problem of Ontogenesis”
- The associated milieu makes functioning and its enabling conditions mutually relevant. Technical openness adds something especially valuable for replay: construction can anticipate maintenance, replacement, and extension. Simondon explicitly discusses relatively detachable subsets, so integration need not mean irreversible fusion. “Technical Mentality”

This gives BWR a substantial philosophical precedent. Hoapa’s particular requirements concerning documentation, accountability, and participant standing remain its own commitments. “Simondon helps us formulate the problem” is stronger and more accurate than claiming he already supplied Hoapa’s solution.

8. How to present the argument without cheating

Begin with the engineered computer, where the interface is easiest to see. Introduce neural AI to show that operational organization can itself be learned. Bring in nervous systems to show how learning and the maintenance of its conditions become intertwined. Then extract the common question: how can work leave behind something that increases the possibilities of further work?

Keep the empirical mechanism, Hoapa’s interpretation, and its normative demand distinguishable. The examples support the possibility and significance of these organizational passages. They do not prove Hoapa’s adequacy, optimality, or political commitments.

The decisive transfer to communities is this: Hoapa seeks to let knowledge acquire increasingly powerful organization without making that organization a classification of the people who produced it. Its documents may become infrastructure for later inquiry; their authors retain lives and standing beyond any function their contributions perform. That is where the biological analogy stops being evidence and Hoapa’s own design responsibility begins.

## Tentative list of mathematical structures which have both kinds of decomposition

It is the first chapter of the secondary thesis: Essai sur l’unité des sciences mathématiques dans leur développement actuel (1938), “Structure d’un domaine de grandeurs et décomposition de ses éléments.” It occupies pp. 89–98 in the 2006 Vrin collection and pp. 50–59 in Duffy’s English translation. French contents, English text.

The distinction is:

- Analytic / proper decomposition: reveals constituents characteristic of the particular being—its prime factors, roots, poles.
- Synthetic / dimensional decomposition: articulates beings according to a common scheme supplied by their domain—a basis, for example.

The proper decomposition remains relative to a domain: changing the coefficient field can change factorization. Lautman’s culminating example is meromorphic functions on a Riemann surface, where Riemann–Roch connects individual singularity data with the dimensional organization of a space of functions. Lautman, chapter I.

There is no mathematically defined class whose members could be exhaustively listed as “all Lautmanian structures.” What follows is a broad inventory of notable families. The classifications beyond his examples are my extensions of his distinction, with the broader ones identified separately.

The closest examples retain an actual coordinate or basis decomposition alongside a decomposition into particular constituents.

| Structure | Synthetic / dimensional decomposition | Analytic / proper decomposition |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Polynomial rings | Expansion in the common monomial basis, or another chosen polynomial basis. | Factorization into irreducibles; over an algebraically closed field, into linear factors determined by roots and multiplicities. |
| 2. Rings of algebraic integers | Additive expansion in a fixed integral basis. | Multiplicative factorization into irreducibles, possibly nonunique. Passing to nonzero ideals restores unique prime-ideal factorization, but changes the objects being decomposed. |
| 3. Rational functions | For a fixed denominator and degree bound, expansion in a common finite basis, such as zʲ/q(z). | Partial fractions organized by the function’s actual poles, together with its polynomial part. |
| 4. Entire functions | Taylor expansion in the shared powers 1, z, z², …, with convergence on compact subsets. | Weierstrass factorization organized by the function’s zeros. The zero-free exponential factor must also be retained. |
| 5. Meromorphic functions on a compact Riemann surface | Expansion in a basis of L(D), the space whose allowed poles are bounded by a fixed effective divisor D. | Articulation through the function’s actual principal parts at its poles, subject to global compatibility and with an additive constant left undetermined. |
| 6. Hardy spaces, especially H² of the disk | Expansion in the common orthonormal basis 1, z, z², … . | Factorization into a Blaschke product, a singular inner factor, and an outer factor, recording the particular function’s zeros and boundary behavior. |
| 7. Finite-dimensional linear operators | Matrix expansion A = Σ aᵢⱼEᵢⱼ in fixed matrix units. | Primary decomposition and Jordan blocks; for normal complex operators, spectral decomposition into eigenspaces. |
| 8. Quadratic and bilinear forms | Coefficients relative to a fixed basis of the underlying vector space. | Orthogonal decomposition into simpler forms: diagonal squares, signature over ℝ, or hyperbolic and anisotropic parts in Witt theory. The hypotheses depend on the field. |
| 9. Matrices and tensors | Expansion in a fixed product basis. | SVD for matrices, Schmidt decomposition for bipartite vectors, and decompositions into decomposable tensors in higher order. Higher tensor decompositions generally lack SVD’s uniqueness properties. |
| 10. Hilbert–Schmidt operators and square-integrable kernels | Expansion in matrix units or product functions supplied by fixed orthonormal bases. | Singular-value expansion into rank-one operators determined by the particular operator; spectral expansion in the self-adjoint case. |

For the mathematical qualifications in this table, see Conrad on ideal factorization, Taylor on entire-function factorization, Yattselev and Baratchart on Hardy-space factorization, Strang on SVD, and Faris on Hilbert–Schmidt operators.

A particularly transparent instance is the polynomial

z² − 3z + 2 = 2·1 − 3·z + 1·z² = (z − 1)(z − 2).

The first expression uses the common dimensional scheme of polynomials of degree at most two. The second exposes this polynomial’s particular roots. The same being supports both articulations.

The following families extend the synthetic side from a basis expansion to a common scheme of presentation or assembly. They are substantial analogues, but this extension should remain explicit.

| Structure | Synthetic / common scheme | Analytic / particular constituents |
|---|---|---|
| 11. Finitely generated modules over a PID, including finitely generated abelian groups | Presentation by a matrix between free modules, expressed in chosen bases. | Free and cyclic torsion summands, organized by invariant factors or elementary divisors. Smith normal form connects the two descriptions. |
| 12. Finite-dimensional representations of groups and algebras | Representation matrices on a fixed vector space, using the same coordinate scheme for all representations of that dimension. | Irreducible summands when semisimplicity holds; otherwise indecomposable summands under Krull–Schmidt. Composition factors alone lose extension data. |
| 13. Quiver representations and finite persistence modules | Vector spaces at prescribed vertices and linear maps along prescribed arrows. | Indecomposable representations; for a finite linearly ordered persistence index, interval modules—the barcode. The quiver supplies the shared organization; the intervals characterize the particular module. |
| 14. Finite-dimensional associative algebras | Multiplication expressed by structure constants on a fixed vector space. | For semisimple algebras, Artin–Wedderburn decomposition into matrix algebras over division algebras. A general algebra also has radical and extension structure that this decomposition does not capture. |
| 15. Finite-dimensional Lie algebras | The bracket expressed in a fixed basis by structure constants. | Simple ideals in the semisimple case; in characteristic zero, Levi decomposition into a semisimple subalgebra acting on the solvable radical. The latter is a semidirect product, with the action retained. |
| 16. Vector bundles, particularly on ℙ¹ | Local frames and transition matrices satisfying a common gluing scheme. | On ℙ¹, Birkhoff–Grothendieck splitting into line bundles O(aᵢ). On more general bases, splitting requires additional hypotheses and may fail. |
| 17. Affine algebraic sets and Noetherian ideals | Equations in a common polynomial coordinate ring; their coefficients use its shared monomial scheme. | Irreducible components geometrically, or primary decomposition of the defining ideal. Primary decomposition retains more scheme structure and need not be unique. |
| 18. Finite graphs and permutations | A graph’s adjacency entries, or a permutation’s matrix entries, relative to fixed labels. | Connected components of the graph; disjoint cycles of the permutation. These are elementary examples of constituents determined by relations rather than by the chosen coordinates. |

The underlying results are documented in Smith normal form and module structure, Etingof et al., representation theory, Levi decomposition, Birkhoff–Grothendieck splitting, and primary decomposition.

Three further families deserve inclusion, with stronger interpretive qualifications:

19. Invariant probability measures and dynamical systems. A fixed family of observables supplies common coordinates for measures; ergodic decomposition expresses a particular invariant measure as an integral of ergodic measures. For a continuous transformation of a compact metric space, the representing measure on ergodic measures is unique. This extends “dimensional decomposition” to a common system of measurements, rather than a literal vector basis. Ergodic decomposition, theorem 4.8.
20. Three-dimensional manifolds. Triangulations assemble manifolds from common simplex types; prime decomposition exposes the particular manifold’s connected-sum factors. JSJ decomposition supplies a further articulation along tori under its appropriate hypotheses. Calling triangulation synthetic extends Lautman’s common scheme from linear coordinates to gluing standard pieces; the gluing data are essential. Hatcher, chapter 1.
21. Presheaves and simplicial sets. The density theorem expresses every presheaf as a canonical colimit of representables: the ambient category supplies the common elementary objects. Separately, a presheaf decomposes into connected summands, indexed by the connected components of its category of elements. For simplicial sets, the first description uses standard simplices and the second connected components. This is a precise categorical candidate for extending the distinction, though a colimit presentation has relations and is not an independent basis expansion. Leinster, §6.2, density theorem.

Riemann–Roch is especially valuable because it explains the compatibility between the two decompositions. Fix a compact connected Riemann surface X and an effective divisor D. Proposed principal parts define a global meromorphic function precisely when their residue pairings with every holomorphic differential vanish. When a function exists, it is determined up to a constant. Meanwhile, the space of all such functions satisfies

dim L(D) − dim L(K − D) = deg D + 1 − g,

where K is a canonical divisor and g the genus. The global structure thus controls which individual singularity data can coexist and how many independent functions they permit. Principal-parts formulation of Riemann–Roch.

Two limits matter when using this inventory:

- The distinction depends on what is being decomposed. Finding an operator’s eigenspaces is proper analysis of that operator. Once fixed, those eigenspaces can furnish a synthetic scheme for every vector on which it acts.
- Two named decompositions do not automatically give the pair. Fourier, wavelet, and Hodge decompositions can all be synthetic relative to fixed ambient data. Likewise, “sheaf,” “topos,” “adjunction,” or “HoTT” alone does not establish both modes: one must identify the objects, their particular constituents, and the common scheme that articulates the family.
