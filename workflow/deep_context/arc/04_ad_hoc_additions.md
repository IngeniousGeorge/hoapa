## Instigator

› So, at some point in a lecture Olivia Caramello said she used the power of toposes to prove a difficult theorem in a specialized mathematical topic, without knowing much about this topic, simply
by using the bridges of topos theory. The way I feel personally, is that toposes exist as mathematical objects, but also as interdicsciplinary objects (like categories in genral, they're on the
boundary between math and the "rest of the universe of Nature-inquiry", but even more so), and what I'm doing with this project/framework is using this "action at a distance" capability to
demonstrate something tangible about all sorts of systems, by saying that if they have property A, then B will apply. Where A is a form of complexity threshold, and B is care. (Like how biology
produced huge muscular cold blooded dinosaurs for a long time, but eventually settled on versatile fluffy mammals after some tinkering.) And then that B/care gives you evolution, which is "what
infinity wants" (here I'm influenced by John Barrow's popular book on the subject). Does any of that sound rational to you?

## Codex

• Yes, but only if you keep two layers distinct.

As a philosophical program, it sounds rational: toposes as bridge-machines, not merely objects. Caramello’s point is exactly that if two theories present the same topos, or are related by the right
geometric bridge, then structure can migrate across domains without requiring local mastery of each domain. That is a disciplined version of “action at a distance.”

Your version becomes rational if:

- A = complexity threshold is made structural, not vague: a system has enough internal differentiation, boundary maintenance, memory, perspective, and composition that local actions can affect
global coherence.
- B = care is made operational, not sentimental: care is the activity/structure that preserves viable continuation across those dependencies. It notices fragility, repairs breaks, respects
boundaries, maintains channels, and keeps futures open.
- Evolution then is not “progress toward goodness,” but the repeated discovery that systems above a certain complexity cannot persist by force alone. They need maintenance, responsiveness, and
repair.

The dinosaur/mammal metaphor works as poetry, but I would be cautious with it as biology. Evolution did not simply “settle” on mammals; birds are dinosaurs, and many lineages thrive by other
strategies. But the underlying point is defensible: at some scales, versatility, warmth, care, sociality, and plasticity become powerful evolutionary strategies.

The phrase “what infinity wants” is rational only as metaphysics or regulative image, not as a literal scientific claim. I’d translate it as: open-ended processes tend to favor structures that
preserve further possibility. Care is then not moral decoration; it is the local form of keeping possibility alive.

In your framework, that maps surprisingly well:

- Modal side: care as cohesion/descent/covering, the ability to hold local perspectives together without crushing them.
- Concurrency side: care as typed interaction, duality, boundary-respect, continuation, repair of communication.
- Dessins/Belyi: finite combinatorics that somehow remember vast arithmetic/geometric worlds.

So yes: rational, if sharpened. The main risk is over-teleology. The strongest version is not “the universe wants care,” but:

> Whenever complexity crosses the point where persistence depends on coherent interaction among perspectives, care becomes a structural necessity.

That is a serious thesis. It is also close to what your Agda project is trying to make tangible.
